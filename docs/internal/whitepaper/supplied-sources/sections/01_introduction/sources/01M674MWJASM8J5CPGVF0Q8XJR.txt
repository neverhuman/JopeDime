\section{Can Machines Invent?}\label{sec:intro}
% Reader question: Can an AI create knowledge no human wrote down, and can that ability be learned rather than rented through test-time search?
% Core points advanced: (1) Innovation is a game; (2) Novelty is free, judgment is the inventor; (3) Search = 1; (4) Skeptics are right about imitation and wrong about learning.
% Speaking points: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8

Can a machine create knowledge that no human ever wrote down? Leading researchers argue that autoregressive language models cannot: trained to predict the next token in a human corpus, they recombine the record of past knowledge rather than extending it. Yet Go once faced the same objection, and AlphaGo Zero answered it by replacing imitation with a referee, playing itself until its neural network alone—without lookahead tree search—played near world-champion strength \citep{silver2017mastering}. We test whether algorithm invention can have its Zero moment by organizing innovation as a game, learning judgment through algorithmic self-play, and emitting a complete learning algorithm for an unseen problem in a single generation (\searchone{}).

\subsection{The Skeptics' Challenge and the Gravity of History}\label{sub:intro-skeptics}

A generator trained solely to imitate human text inherits the ceiling of its training distribution. In March 2025, \citet{lecun2025bigtech} argued that autoregressive language models, ``in their purest form, they are incapable of inventing new things,'' because they retrieve and interpolate existing solutions rather than discovering new ones. In the same month, \citet{wolf2025einstein} warned that optimizing models to match existing literature risks building ``a country of yes-men on servers'' rather than scientific innovators. First posing the question to Dario Amodei in August 2023, \citet{patel2023question} asked why models with ``basically the entire corpus of human knowledge memorized'' had not yet made ``a single new connection that has led to a discovery.'' Even \citet{hassabis2025bigtech} framed the benchmark gap in January 2025 by noting that current systems ``can play a game of Go at a world champion level. But could a system invent Go?''

These critiques identify a real constraint, but they diagnose a property of imitation rather than a permanent limit of learned systems. Producing a new candidate is trivial: a random string generator produces unprecedented sequences endlessly, and Borges's Library of Babel already contains every program that will ever be written \citep{borges1941babel}. What distinguishes an inventor from a random generator is not novelty, which is free, but judgment—knowing before execution which unfamiliar candidate will actually work. When a model is trained by next-token prediction on historical code and papers, it absorbs history's taste: it assigns high probability to familiar patterns and low probability to departures from consensus.

Human researchers face the same historical gravity. Across industries and scientific disciplines, ideas are getting harder to find: research effort rises sharply while measured research productivity falls \citep{bloom2020ideas}. Aspiring inventors face a growing burden of knowledge, requiring longer training just to reach the existing frontier \citep{jones2009burden}. Over recent decades, published papers and patents have become systematically less disruptive \citep{park2023disruptive}, and empirical studies of scientific subfields show that established paradigms hold back outside ideas until dominant incumbents depart—science advances one funeral at a time \citep{kuhn1962structure,azoulay2019funeral}. The bottleneck in discovery is not a shortage of variations; it is the pull of what we already know.

\subsection{Scaling Search versus Learning Taste}\label{sub:intro-search}

To escape the prior of a single forward pass, the field has converged on scaling test-time search. Systems such as FunSearch \citep{romeraparedes2024funsearch} and AlphaEvolve \citep{novikov2025alphaevolve} pair a language model with an evolutionary verifier, evaluating thousands of mutated programs per problem. Agentic research loops—including the AI Scientist \citep{lu2024aiscientist}, the AI co-scientist \citep{gottweis2025coscientist}, and \citet{karpathy2026autoresearch}'s \texttt{autoresearch} in March 2026—run dozens to hundreds of automated experiments overnight and retain the winning artifacts. At larger scale, OpenAI set an October 2025 target of an automated research intern running across massive GPU clusters \citep{altman2025livestream,openai2026research}, while \citet{socher2026eureka}'s \emph{The Eureka Machine} envisions ``millions of AI agents'' that ``critique each other's ideas,'' backed by a May 2026 launch at Recursive raising \$\num{650} million to let AI systems ``go back and forth for millions of iterations'' \citep{recursive2026techcrunch,recursive2026research}.\footnote{Several lines of work share the Eureka name, all descending in spirit from \citet{lenat1983eurisko}'s EURISKO: NVIDIA's Eureka uses coding models for reinforcement-learning reward design \citep{ma2024eureka}, Karpathy's Eureka Labs focuses on human education, and \citet{socher2026eureka}'s Eureka Machine orchestrates parallel research agents across knowledge, simulation, and laboratory pillars.}

\begin{keyidea}
Everyone else is buying judgment by the try. We learn it once.
\end{keyidea}

These search systems demonstrate that pairing a generator with an external evaluator yields verified discoveries, and their evaluators supply essential infrastructure for automated science. Yet whenever a search swarm faces a new problem without updating the generator's weights, it pays the full price of judgment again at test time. What the swarm learns stays in its output logs rather than its proposal policy: search rents intelligence, whereas learning owns it. If each candidate beats the reigning algorithm on a problem with probability $q$, an unguided or frozen searcher must evaluate $\mathbb{E}[N_{\text{tries}}] = 1/q$ candidates on average to find a winner. Raising $q$ so that the first candidate succeeds requires moving judgment upstream into the policy itself—a capability we call \emph{taste}.

\subsection{The Zero Move for Invention}\label{sub:intro-zero}

Go demonstrated how a learner acquires taste from a referee. AlphaGo first combined supervised learning on human games with heavy Monte Carlo tree search, yet during its 2016 match against Lee Sedol it played Move 37—a move a human professional would choose with probability roughly one in \num{10000}, which nevertheless proved decisive \citep{silver2016mastering,metz2016twomoves}. A year later, AlphaGo Zero discarded human games entirely, playing \num{4.9} million games of self-play in three days until it defeated AlphaGo Lee \num{100}--0 \citep{silver2017mastering}. Crucially, when evaluated with tree search disabled, AlphaGo Zero's raw neural network achieved an Elo rating of \num{3055}, nearly matching the \num{3144} Elo of the search-assisted AlphaGo Fan that had defeated a professional player \citep{silver2017mastering}. Through expert iteration, extensive training-time search had been distilled into single-move intuition \citep{anthony2017expert}.

Human expertise operates by the same compression. Cognitive studies of chess masters show that grandmasters do not calculate deeper trees than weaker players; their trained perception recognizes strong moves first \citep{degroot1965thought,chase1973perception}. As \citet{simon1992explanation} observed, expert intuition is ``nothing more and nothing less than recognition.''

We ask whether algorithm discovery can make the same transition from test-time search to learned intuition (\cref{fig:empty-square}). We formalize innovation as a game $\Game = (\Prob, \encP, \Sol, \encS, \referee, \reward, \opp)$ in which the encoded problem and reigning algorithm form the board, a complete typed program is the move, an empirical verifier on hidden folds is the referee, and the ratcheting incumbent is the opponent. By training a program writer $\writer$ alongside a contrastive problem--program map $\mapv$ through algorithmic self-play, \EZ{} internalizes the referee's verdicts. \EZ{} is to the Eureka Machine what AlphaGo Zero was to AlphaGo: it turns a multi-trial search loop into a trained policy that acts in one move (\cref{box:search-one}).

\begin{explainer}{Box 1: What ``\searchone{}'' means}\label{box:search-one}
On game day, \EZ{} receives a new problem and emits a single complete algorithm candidate ($B = 1$):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{One candidate, one generation:} A single autoregressive generation process produces one executable program. There are no retries, compile-and-repair loops, best-of-$N$ sampling, candidate reranking, or test-time weight updates.
  \item \textbf{All inference compute counted:} Problem encoding ($\encP$) and autoregressive reasoning tokens are logged as part of the single move's deployment cost.
  \item \textbf{Generation versus fitting:} The emitted machine-learning algorithm is subsequently fit to the problem's permitted training split, exactly as any human-designed estimator is fit before predicting on held-out data.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Zero search, not zero knowledge:} \EZ{} starts from a pretrained code model and human mathematical primitives, then learns from self-play so that test-time search drops to one.
\end{explainer}

Consider a concrete deployment episode on a held-out problem from our sealed evaluation (\TBD{dataset name, the median win}): the system observes only the leak-free problem encoding $z_p = \encP(p)$, generates one readable program $w \sim \writer(\cdot \mid z_p)$ in \TBD{G} seconds without testing alternatives, and fits that program on the training rows. When the strongest frontier model is run as an iterative agent on the same unseen problems \citep{karpathy2026autoresearch}, it requires \TBD{R} evaluated tries per problem to match \EZ{}'s first move.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{The anatomy of innovation (\cref{sec:anatomy}):} We decompose discovery across scientific traditions into four pillars—aim, variation, judgment, and retention—and show that expected search length is the reciprocal of learned taste, $\mathbb{E}[N_{\text{tries}}] = 1/q$ (\cref{eq:taste}).
  \item \textbf{The innovation game (\cref{sec:road,sec:game}):} After tracing how five lines of AI research built each pillar individually (\cref{sec:road}), we formalize invention as a playable game $\Game$ whose board is any encoded problem with a verifier (\cref{sec:game}).
  \item \textbf{The economics of algorithmic self-play (\cref{sec:price}):} Modeling training costs across open-weight and frontier architectures from 8B to \num{1.5}T parameters, we show that wasted moves—stillborn programs and also-rans—dominate the bill rather than token prices (\cref{eq:bill}).
  \item \textbf{\EZ{} (\cref{sec:method}):} We introduce a self-play architecture that pairs a program writer $\writer$ with a contrastive problem--program map $\mapv$ (\cref{eq:mapLoss}), steering generation away from stillborn regions and toward winners.
  \item \textbf{Single-move evidence (\cref{sec:results,sec:meaning}):} On \TBD{M} unseen problems, we benchmark \EZ{} at \searchone{} against algorithms invented by Claude, Codex, and Gemini, against KumoRFM, and against multi-try agentic search (\cref{sec:results}), before mapping how the same game recipe extends to any discipline with a faithful referee (\cref{sec:meaning,sec:conclusion}).
\end{enumerate}
