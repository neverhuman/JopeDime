% Abstract
\begin{izabstract}
\noindent{\sffamily\sbseries\small\color{izaccent}Abstract}\par\smallskip
\noindent In 2016, AlphaGo played a move its own imitation network rated at one chance in ten
thousand, and won with it; the move came from search. The best-known machine discoveries since, from faster
matrix multiplication to new mathematical constructions, were likewise found by generating and
testing candidates for the problem at hand. We ask whether that iteration can be learned once and
then removed: can a model read a dataset and propose a new, working algorithm for it \emph{before
any candidate has been executed}? We make the question precise. \emph{Zero} is a set of counters
--- one committed proposal, no candidate executions, no revisions --- and we separate three tracks:
a universal algorithm written without data (U), a data-conditioned program found with a declared
evaluation budget (S), and a conditional first proposal with none (C), which is the InnovationZero
setting. For track U we built the General Regressor Challenge, a closed-world, reasoning-only
contract, and gave it to four frontier language models. They succeed: each wrote a complete,
dependency-free Rust regressor, and on a 64-family panel the best ranks well above the rest
(Opus~5.5 at Elo~1641 against 1342 for the weakest) and is the best of the four on 30 families ---
yet every entrant is the best somewhere, and error magnitudes separate only the weakest. By their reported or estimated authoring
receipts, the three sessions with records kept 10--15\% of the tokens they processed and delivered
about ten tokens of algorithm a second. All four designs are variations on one textbook hybrid of
ridge regression and gradient-boosted trees. For
track S, a program-synthesis engine writes a different standalone program for each dataset in
seconds; on ten datasets with identical cuts it is not significantly different from the best
frontier entrant in this small exploratory panel and above default gradient-boosting libraries. The objective is also part of the input: implemented objective-aware selection, cross-fitted calibration and program ranking provide baselines for an objective-conditioned proposer. Track C has not been run, and we
prove that the engine's existing catalog-bound neural policy cannot, by construction, produce a
program outside its catalog. We therefore contribute the setting, the challenge, a frontier comparison, a pilot, a
token-yield accounting that compares where systems explore rather than how much they print, and a
pre-specified decisive experiment --- including an operational definition of \emph{Move~42}, a
held-out-useful algorithm beyond retrieval whose mechanism survives ablation.
\end{izabstract}
