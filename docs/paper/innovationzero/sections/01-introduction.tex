% Section 1 — Introduction
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{Move 37} On 10 March 2016, in the second game of its match against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo
placed a stone on the fifth line that commentators first took for a mistake. Fan Hui, the European
champion the program had beaten months earlier, was blunt: ``It's not a human move. I've never seen
a human play this move'' \citep{metz2016wired}. Lee left the table and took roughly a quarter of an
hour to reply. AlphaGo's policy network, trained to imitate strong human players, had put the chance
that a person would play the move at about one in ten thousand \citep{hassabis2016seoul}. The
program played it anyway, because its search said it would win. It won that game and the match,
four games to one.

Three days later Lee answered with Move~78, a wedge that professionals called ``God's touch''
\citep{koreaherald2016}, and AlphaGo's network had rated that move at the same one in ten thousand
\citep{hassabis2016seoul}. Lee won the game. Invention ran in both directions that week, and in
both directions it registered the same way: as an event the imitation model considered nearly
impossible.

It is worth being exact about where Move~37 came from. The component of AlphaGo that had learned
from people thought it unthinkable; the component that evaluated consequences found it. The
creativity was in the search, and the search was paid for at the moment of play.

\paragraph{From imitation to amortization} Nineteen months later AlphaGo Zero removed the human
games entirely \citep{silver2017zero}. Starting from random play and the rules, it learned by
playing itself: each new network was trained to predict the moves its own search preferred and the
games it went on to win. After three days it beat the version that had defeated Lee Sedol by 100
games to none. One result in that paper matters more to us than its headline. With no search at
all --- a single forward pass, playing the network's preferred move --- AlphaGo Zero rated about
3,000 Elo, close to the version that had first beaten a professional; with search it rated 5,185
\citep{silver2017zero}. A large share of what search had discovered had been \emph{amortized} into
the network. The rest still needed search at the moment of play.

The lineage that followed pointed the same recipe at harder kinds of discovery
(\cref{fig:timeline}). AlphaZero generalized it across chess, shogi and Go
\citep{silver2018alphazero}; MuZero learned the rules themselves \citep{schrittwieser2020muzero};
AlphaTensor found a way to multiply $4\times4$ matrices in arithmetic modulo 2 with 47
multiplications, improving on Strassen's 49 \citep{fawzi2022alphatensor}; AlphaDev found sorting
routines that were merged into the LLVM C\texttt{++} standard library
\citep{mankowitz2023alphadev}; FunSearch, wrapping a language model in a loop of proposal and
evaluation, improved the asymptotic lower bound for cap sets \citep{romeraparedes2024funsearch};
and AlphaEvolve found a 48-multiplication algorithm for $4\times4$ complex-valued matrices and a
scheduling heuristic that recovers about 0.7\% of Google's worldwide compute
\citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}. Each was a genuine discovery. Each was found the same way: by
generating and evaluating thousands to millions of candidates \emph{for the problem at hand}.

\paragraph{The objection} Language models were built on the opposite principle. They learn from
what people wrote, and then from what people prefer \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt}. Their critics
argue that this is the wrong objective for discovery. Yann LeCun wrote in 2023 that ``On the
highway towards Human-Level AI, Large Language Model is an off-ramp'' \citep{lecun2023offramp},
and in 2025 that in their purest form they are ``incapable of inventing new things''
\citep{lecun2025bigtech}; in 2026, founding a company on the alternative, he put it more simply:
``The breakthroughs are not going to come from scaling up LLMs'' \citep{lecun2026mittr}. Richard
Sutton argues that a language model can predict what a person would say but not what will happen,
because ``There's no ground truth in large language models'' \citep{sutton2025dwarkesh}. Thomas
Wolf calls today's assistants ``a country of yes-men on servers'' and, pointedly, describes Move~37
itself as ``essentially a straight-A student answer'' \citep{wolf2025einstein}. Demis Hassabis sets
the bar higher still: the test of machine creativity is not a brilliant move but whether a system
could invent a game as deep as Go \citep{hassabis2025bigtech,mccracken2026alphago}. And Diogo
Almeida, an author of the instruction-following work that made assistants useful, now argues that
training on human preference is the wrong target for systems that must act alone
\citep{almeida2026aie,typesafe2026jev}.

The counter-evidence is just as real. Frontier models now reach certified gold-medal standard at the
International Mathematical Olympiad \citep{deepmind2025imo} and write large working programs; inside
evaluator loops such as AlphaEvolve's, or the automated research loop with which Recursive reports
building its Flint model \citep{recursive2026flint}, they contribute to discoveries. Richard Socher's
\emph{The Eureka Machine} \citep{socher2026eureka} extends that loop to all of science: a model of
the world's knowledge, a model of reality learned from measurement, high-fidelity simulation and
autonomous laboratories, with swarms of agents proposing and criticizing ideas across them
\citep{socher2026bookbite}. The disagreement, then, is not really about whether language models help
discovery. It is about \emph{where the discovery comes from}: from the model, or from the loop
wrapped around it.

\paragraph{A test without a loop} To separate the two we removed the loop. In the General Regressor
Challenge (\cref{sec:challenge}) a frontier model receives a forty-kilobyte contract and must write, by
reasoning alone, the strongest general regression algorithm it can --- in dependency-free Rust, with
no data, no execution and no feedback, frozen once and ranked by held-out error on datasets it will
never see. Four frontier-model entrants did it,\footnote{The entrants are named for the models that
wrote them. Only Opus~5.5's configured model identity is recorded; the GPT-6 Astra session reported
its own model string, and the files submitted as GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini carry no provider
telemetry. \Cref{tab:entrants,app:receipts} give each receipt as written.} and every program
worked. On a 64-family panel the best of them, written by Opus~5.5, ranks clearly above the
others; on ten datasets with identical cuts, the original three all rank above the default settings
of XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost by Elo, though only Opus's margins are statistically clear
(\cref{sec:evidence}). Reasoning in words can produce a working new algorithm. But the receipts show
what it costs and where it goes: the three sessions with records kept 10--15\% of the tokens they
processed, and all four designs are variations on one hybrid --- ridge regression plus
gradient-boosted trees, blended on held-out rows --- that is the consensus answer of a decade of
tabular machine learning \citep{grinsztajn2022trees}. Asked to invent, the models reconstructed the
textbook.

\paragraph{The question} Every route to machine discovery above pays for search when the problem
arrives. AlphaGo Zero's raw network suggests another possibility: that much of that search can be
paid for in advance, across many problems, and carried in a model that proposes well the first
time. Algorithms are not Go positions --- every dataset is a new board, the space of programs is
unbounded, and winning means predicting rows nobody has seen --- and nobody knows whether this kind
of amortization is possible for them. If it is, the economics of discovery change: the loop does
not disappear, it moves upstream into training, where it runs once over every dataset the system
has met.

\begin{izquestion}
Can a model read a dataset it has never seen and propose a new, working algorithm for it in one
move --- before a single candidate has been executed? And if it can, what is the first algorithm it
writes that a frontier model would not: its Move~42?
\end{izquestion}

\paragraph{Contributions} This paper does not answer that question yet; it makes it answerable, and
reports what can already be measured.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A precise setting} (\cref{sec:setting}). We define \emph{zero} by counters --- one
committed proposal, no candidate executions, no revisions --- distinguish universal (U),
search-assisted (S) and conditional first-proposal (C) tracks, and prove that a decoder whose
support is a fixed catalog cannot produce a program outside it, however it is trained.
\item \textbf{The General Regressor Challenge} (\cref{sec:challenge} and the archive volume): a
neutral, closed-world, red-teamed contract for invention by reasoning, with four frozen frontier
entrants and their authoring receipts.
\item \textbf{An architecture and game for track C} (\cref{sec:model}): a 512-dimensional problem
encoding, a protected \emph{kernel embedding} described by the guarantees it enforces, and the
outcome-based objectives used to train it.
\item \textbf{A frontier comparison} (\cref{sec:frontier}, \cref{app:frontier}): the four
frontier-written algorithms on 64 dataset families, by Elo, error magnitude and family-by-family
wins, beside the \emph{token yield} and tokens per second of the sessions that wrote them --- an
accounting of machine invention that measures where a system explores, with the receipts'
inconsistencies shown.
\item \textbf{A pilot on identical cuts} (\cref{sec:evidence}): the frontier entrants against a
data-conditioned engine and standard libraries --- including results that do not favour our own
system.
\item \textbf{A decisive experiment, specified in advance} (\cref{sec:protocol}), with a claim
ladder and an operational definition of Move~42.
\end{enumerate}
