\section{The setting: what ``zero'' means}
\label{sec:setting}

``Zero-shot'' is used loosely enough that we define it by counting. A dataset $D$ is split into
training rows $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and held-out rows $D_{\mathrm{te}}$, the target always in the last
column. A \emph{proposer} $P$ reads whatever evidence its track permits and \emph{commits} a program
$a$: source code together with every configuration, constant and version that determines its
behaviour. Only then is the program fitted and scored:
\begin{equation}
w = \mathrm{Fit}_a(D_{\mathrm{tr}}), \qquad \hat y = \mathrm{Predict}_a(X_{\mathrm{te}};\, w),
\qquad \text{outcome} = \ell(\hat y, y_{\mathrm{te}}).
\label{eq:commit}
\end{equation}
Here $w$ is the fitted state of the \emph{program} (ridge coefficients, tree splits), not the
weights of the proposer. Held-out labels $y_{\mathrm{te}}$ never reach $P$.

\begin{definition}{The commitment contract}
\label{def:zero}
A proposal is \emph{zero-feedback} when, for the dataset at hand,
\begin{itemize}
\item $K=1$: exactly one complete program is committed; no rejection sampling, no reranking among
complete candidates, no best-of-$B$;
\item $E=0$: no candidate is executed, compiled for feedback, or scored before commitment;
\item $R=0$: nothing is revised after any feedback; a repaired program is a new attempt, and the
first attempt's failure stands.
\end{itemize}
Permitted: computing an encoding of $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$, any number of internal decoding steps
(each counted and reported), and fitting the committed program afterwards. A program may itself
contain internal model selection inside $\mathrm{Fit}$; such programs satisfy the contract but are
labelled \emph{search-in-fit}, and a claim of ``no search at all'' requires a cohort that forbids
it. Counters are recorded by the supervisor, never taken from the proposer's own report.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Three tracks} The contract lets us separate three settings that are usually blurred
(\cref{tab:tracks}). In \textbf{track U} (universal), the proposer sees no data at all and writes
one program for every dataset; a frontier model answering the General Regressor Challenge is a
track-U proposer with $K=1$, $E=0$, $R=0$. In \textbf{track S} (search-assisted), the proposer
reads $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and may execute up to $k$ candidates on it before committing; the engine
whose programs we report in \cref{sec:evidence} is a track-S proposer with $k=256$. In
\textbf{track C} (conditional first proposal), the proposer reads $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and commits once
with $E=0$. Track C is the InnovationZero setting, and it is the one this paper does not yet have
results for.

\paragraph{A limit on catalog-bound decoders} The most natural way to build a track-C proposer is
to train a network to choose among programs that worked before. The engine's first neural policy
does exactly that: it decodes inside a prefix tree built from a fixed catalog $\mathcal{C}$ of
6,640 verified programs, renormalizing its logits over the children of each prefix
\citep{jopedime2026writeup}. Such a decoder can be excellent at selection. It cannot invent.

\begin{proposition}{Closed support}
\label{prop:closed}
If the support of $\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid z)$ is contained in a fixed set
$\mathcal{C}$ for every permitted encoding $z$, then $\Pr_{a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid z)}[a\notin
\mathcal{C}]=0$ for every $\theta$ the training procedure can reach.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Proof.} Probability outside the support is zero by definition, and no update to $\theta$ that
keeps the decoding mask changes the support. \hfill\tikz\draw[line width=0.5pt] (0,0) rectangle (0.62em,0.62em);
\par\smallskip
\emph{Consequence.} Novelty relative to $\mathcal{C}$ requires a proposer whose support extends
beyond it; better optimization, more data or a better reward cannot substitute. A catalog-bound
policy is a \emph{control} for track C --- the selection baseline that open synthesis must beat ---
not a candidate for it. A membership test against $\mathcal{C}$, published without the catalog
itself, is enough for readers to audit the claim.
\end{proposition}

The same observation splits the gap between a proposer and the best achievable program into two
parts. Let $U(a)$ be held-out utility, $a^\star_{\mathrm{all}}$ the best program in any language and
$a^\star_{\mathcal{A}}$ the best program the proposer can express. Then
\begin{equation}
U(a^\star_{\mathrm{all}}) - U(\hat a) =
\underset{\text{construction gap}}{\bigl[U(a^\star_{\mathrm{all}}) - U(a^\star_{\mathcal{A}})\bigr]}
+ \underset{\text{policy regret}}{\bigl[U(a^\star_{\mathcal{A}}) - U(\hat a)\bigr]} .
\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
Training reduces only the second term; only a richer construction space reduces the first. A third
loss sits upstream: a proposer that sees $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ only through an encoding $z$ can do no
better in expectation than one that sees $D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ itself, $\mathbb{E}[\sup_a
\mathbb{E}[U\mid z,a]] \le \mathbb{E}[\sup_a \mathbb{E}[U\mid D_{\mathrm{tr}},a]]$. Encoder
information loss, construction-space exclusion and policy regret are therefore separate
bottlenecks, and the protocol in \cref{sec:protocol} measures each.
