\section{The General Regressor Challenge}
\label{sec:challenge}

Arguments about whether language models can invent are usually conducted with anecdotes. We
wanted a measurement: a task with one deliverable, no access to data, no feedback, and a
verdict that does not depend on anyone's taste. The General Regressor Challenge
\citep{jopedime2026challenge} is that task. Its participant packet and the organizer's master file
are reproduced byte for byte in the archive volume that accompanies this paper; this section explains
the design and what four frontier models did with it.

\subsection{Design}

\paragraph{One deliverable, frozen once} An entrant must write \emph{one} complete regression
algorithm in safe Rust (edition 2021, a single file, single-threaded) that will be fitted,
without the entrant's involvement, on thousands of unknown datasets and ranked by Elo on
held-out root-mean-squared error. ``One algorithm'' means one immutable learning procedure:
it may estimate parameters and make internal decisions from its current training rows, but it
may not recognize datasets, defer choices, or ask to be tuned later.

\paragraph{A closed world of fundamentals} Everything not expressly permitted is forbidden.
Entrants may use IEEE-754 binary64 arithmetic; nineteen named scalar functions (from
\code{abs} and \code{sqrt} to \code{erf}, \code{asinh} and \code{sigmoid}); seven row summaries;
arrays, sorting and any linear algebra they implement themselves; and a pseudorandom generator
seeded only from the supplied seed. No external crate, no \code{unsafe}, no filesystem, clock,
thread or network access, and no numerical library: there is no supplied matrix inverse,
solver, optimizer or regression estimator. A function an entrant could have written is not
allowed merely because it could have been written; it must be written.

\paragraph{Design without experiments} The default authoring mode is \emph{reasoning only}: no
tools, no browsing, no code execution, no data. Entrants may not generate synthetic data, run
a candidate on anything, or train a surrogate. An organizer may instead enable a uniform
\emph{compilation-only} mode (at most three compiler requests and two revisions, diagnostics
only). After the source is frozen, the evaluator fits it on training rows; that is its
intended use. The prohibition is on empirical feedback during design, not on learning at
evaluation time.

\paragraph{A fixed contract} The final column of every training table is the target; prediction
inputs contain features only. The callable interface is the trait in \cref{lst:trait}; the
resource contract is one CPU core, \SI{8}{GiB} of memory, \SI{600}{s} per fit and \SI{60}{s}
per prediction partition. Failures, timeouts and non-finite predictions count as losses.

\paragraph{A red-teamed specification} The organizer appendix (Supplement~\ref{S-sup:organizer}) records
sixteen attacks on earlier drafts and how each was closed: an unspecified target column, row
summaries that could leak the label, a wrapper around a foreign implementation, compile-time
evaluation used as a hidden experiment, unequal tool access between entrants, refits that
inherit stale state, and selective omission of failed tasks, among others. Twenty-two
organizer-only correctness checks (shape errors, overflow, missing values, zero features,
lifecycle, batch and permutation invariance, refit isolation) run only after the source is
frozen and return no feedback to the author.

The challenge measures task-time reasoning. It cannot certify that a model never encountered
an idea, or a dataset, during pre-training, and the specification says so in its own words
(Supplement~\ref{S-sup:organizer}). What it can certify is that the frozen program was not shaped by any
data the entrant saw during the task.

\subsection{Entrants}
\label{sec:entrants}

Four entrants have been frozen and integrated into the repository's benchmark harness: three on
21 October 2026 (pull requests \#391, \#392 and \#393) and one on 22 October (pull request \#407,
open at the time of writing). \Cref{tab:entrants} records what each receipt says about its own
authorship. Every entrant compiled at its first post-freeze attempt and none was edited after
freezing. None is a pristine primary entrant, and the receipts say so. Each of the first three
authoring conversations carried earlier project context; Astra's provenance records that the
organizer appendix was in context and that its prompt hash is that of the full 68,744-byte
specification rather than the participant packet, and the specification sizes Opus and Sol report
(about 16--17 thousand tokens) also match the full file. The fourth file was supplied by the
repository owner without authoring telemetry; its name indicates a Gemini model, and it replaced an
earlier Gemini source that is not in the repository. An authoring statement supplied for it later is
a self-report whose token counts do not fit the file, and we treat it as such (\cref{sec:yield}).

\subsection{What they invented}
\label{sec:convergence}

Read side by side, the four designs describe one idea four times (\cref{tab:convergence}). Each
fits a ridge-regularized linear model whose penalty is chosen on rows held out inside the training
call; each fits histogram-binned gradient-boosted trees with explicit handling of missing values;
and each combines its components by minimizing squared error on internal held-out rows, with the
training mean available as a fallback. Opus adds a second boosting family and cross-fitting; Astra
a quadratic feature expansion; Sol starts its trees from the ridge residual; Gemini, the largest
file, stacks five experts including splines, an asinh-target booster and nearest neighbours. These
are real differences in engineering. They are not differences in kind.

That design is, in substance, the consensus answer of the last decade of tabular machine
learning: gradient-boosted trees dominate typical tabular data
\citep{grinsztajn2022trees,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm,prokhorenkova2018catboost}, linear
models are the classic remedy for their poor extrapolation \citep{hoerl1970ridge}, and blending
on held-out error is standard practice. Asked to invent, the models reconstructed the textbook.
We state the limit of this observation plainly: the first three sessions shared project context and
the fourth is undocumented, so their agreement is not independent evidence. It is nonetheless what each model chose when told
that nothing would be recommended to it.

\subsection{What they got right}

It would be easy to read the convergence as a failure. It is not. Writing 25--67 kilobytes of
numerically careful Rust with no compiler, no test and no data, as the three documented entrants
did, and having it compile at the first attempt and pass every contract check, is a feat that would have been unthinkable for any
model five years ago. The entrants handle missing values, zero-feature tables, single rows,
constant targets, overflow-safe target scaling and deterministic refits, all without having run
a line. \Cref{sec:evidence} shows that, on our pilot panel, their predictions are also good. The
question this paper asks is not whether frontier models can write a new working algorithm. They
can. It is where the invention comes from, what it costs, and whether it can come from the data
instead.
