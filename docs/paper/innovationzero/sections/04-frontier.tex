\section{Four frontier inventors, compared}
\label{sec:frontier}

The four frozen programs can now be compared in the only way the challenge allows: by running them,
unchanged, on data none of their authors saw. \Cref{fig:frontier} puts the comparison on one page. It
answers four questions about the same four programs --- how good each is, how much of the text its
session processed survived into the program, how fast that text was produced, and what the program
costs to run --- and it marks, rather than hides, the one answer the receipts cannot support. This
is track U throughout: one general program per model, no data at design time.

\subsection{Quality: who wins, and by how much}
\label{sec:u64}

The panel is 64 dataset families, each with three folds and three seeds, for 576 fits per entrant;
every fit was isolated to one CPU and 8~GiB as in the challenge contract, inputs were byte-identical
across entrants and no cell failed \pre. A family's match outcome between two entrants is the mean
over seeds of a win, loss or tie on RMSE pooled across folds, and the ratings are a Bradley--Terry fit
on those outcomes \citep{bradley1952,elo1978}. Opus5p5\_Extra rates 1641 (95\% interval 1599--1689),
Gemini4\_argon\_high 1561 (1519--1607), GPT6p1\_Sol\_Max 1456 (1414--1497) and GPT6\_Astra\_Max
1342 (1289--1388) (\cref{fig:frontier}a). Adjacent intervals overlap, but each excludes its
neighbours' point estimates.

The ratings rest on head-to-head records that are easy to state. Opus beats Gemini on 60\% of family
matches, Sol on 79\% and Astra on 86\%; Gemini beats Sol on 66\% and Astra on 77\%; Sol beats Astra
on 70\%. Counting every family separately (\cref{app:frontier}), Opus is the best of the four on 30
families, Gemini on 20, Sol on 7 and Astra on 5, with two families shared; Opus sweeps all three of
its matches on all three seeds in 24 families and Astra in one. Every entrant is the best available
general algorithm somewhere. None is the best everywhere.

Elo counts wins, not margins, and the two orderings disagree in an instructive way
(\cref{tab:magnitude}). Sol, third by Elo, has the lowest geometric-mean error of the four --- 4\%
below Opus's, not significantly --- while Gemini, which beats Sol on 65.6\% of family matches, has a
geometric-mean RMSE 18\% \emph{higher} than Sol's at the point estimate (ratio 0.847, interval
0.623--1.055): against Sol, Gemini's wins are small and its losses large. Only Astra is separated
from the others on magnitude, and none of the three Gemini comparisons survives the Holm adjustment
its analysis applies \citep{holm1979}. A paper that reported only the ladder would
rank these inventions; it would not say how much better any of them is.

Three limits travel with these numbers, and the analysis that produced them states each one. The 64
families are current training families with prepared inner-validation contexts, not untouched data;
the stored preprocessing was fitted before the original train/test split; and the first seed's
outcomes were seen before the other two seeds were fixed. The three earlier entrants' 1,728 fits
were reused from one host and Gemini's 576 run on another. The ratings describe these four programs
on this panel, against each other: they do not transfer to another ladder, and they are not
evidence about how a model performs on independent data.

\subsection{Token yield: where the invention happened}
\label{sec:yield}

A frontier model that invents an algorithm by reasoning pays for every idea it considers in tokens:
abandoned approaches, re-reading of the specification, partial drafts and the final source are all
generated or processed text. A program-synthesis model pays for exploration in program evaluations
instead, which cost milliseconds of CPU and produce no text at all. \emph{Token yield} makes that
difference visible. It is not a measure of quality, and it is easy to misuse, so we define it with
care.

Let $S$ be the number of tokens in the frozen source that the system itself generated, $O$ all
visible output generated before freezing, $R$ hidden reasoning tokens not already counted in $O$,
and $I$ the input tokens processed (new and cached reads reported separately, never added twice).
All counts use one stated tokenizer; a count that is not available is \emph{missing}, never zero.

\begin{definition}{Token yield}
\label{def:yield}
\begin{equation*}
Y_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{S}{O}, \qquad
Y_{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{S}{O+R}, \qquad
Y_{\mathrm{proc}} = \frac{S}{I+O+R}.
\end{equation*}
$Y_{\mathrm{gen}}$ asks how much of what the system generated survived into the artifact;
$Y_{\mathrm{proc}}$ charges everything it read as well. When only a session's total content $T$ is
known, we report the \emph{content yield} $Y_{\mathrm{cont}} = S/T$ and say so.
\end{definition}

Two further quantities complete the picture. A system that explores without generating text has no
meaningful token yield; for it we report the \emph{candidate yield}, one over the number of
candidates executed before commitment ($1/k$ for a track-S proposer, $1$ for track C and for
reasoning-only authors), and the \emph{expansion factor} $X = S_{\mathrm{emit}}/N_{\mathrm{dec}}$, the
emitted source per decoded decision. $X$ can exceed one and is not an efficiency: a 512-coordinate
vector is not 512 language-model tokens, and source expanded from it is not evidence of
near-complete discovery efficiency. The repository's own stream metric, compiler-passed tokens over
emitted tokens, measures something else again (compilation and conformance), and we name it
accordingly.

\paragraph{What the receipts say} No frontier session in this study has metered usage; every token
count is the session's own estimate, and source tokens are frozen bytes divided by 3.5 characters
per token (range 3.0--4.0), the heuristic Astra's own receipt uses. \Cref{tab:yield} and
\cref{fig:frontier}b report what can be computed. The three documented sessions kept 10--15\% of the
content they processed: Astra 9.9\% (6.3--15.4), Sol 11.9\% (7.8--18.5) and Opus 14.6\%
(9.2--27.7). Opus, the one session that separates generation from input, kept a quarter of what it
generated (25.3\%, 16.6--44.4). Its integration phase then processed a further 94 thousand output
tokens --- 27 thousand of them reasoning, already included --- 245 thousand new input tokens and 19.5
million cached re-reads; counted all the way to a working integration, about one token in a thousand
that the session read survived into the regressor.

\paragraph{Gemini's statement} After integration the repository owner supplied an authoring statement
for the Gemini entrant (archived verbatim in \code{evidence/gemini-authoring-statement-20261022.md}):
a single \code{reasoning\_only} pass of about 11,800 tokens --- 2,600 reasoning and 9,200 output
across the source, its manifest and a design note --- in an estimated 55--85 seconds, with no
compilation, data or experiments during design. Taken at its word it would make Gemini the outlier
on every axis: up to 78\% of what it generated kept, and 139--215 tokens a second. But the counts do
not fit the file. The archived source alone is 96,694 bytes, 24--32 thousand tokens at 3.0--4.0
characters per token, which is 2.6--3.5 times the output the statement reports for all three files;
at face value it would be 10.5 bytes per token, and with the archived source as numerator its yield
would exceed 100\%. Delivering that source in 55--85 seconds would also mean 284--586 source tokens a
second, twenty to sixty times the documented sessions. The superseded Gemini source is larger still
(98,057 bytes), so the statement does not describe it either. Either its counts are about threefold
low or they describe another generation. We draw its values, open and grey, so that the reader can
see them, and we compare nothing with them until the session is re-run with metering (E0).

\paragraph{The one exception, and why it is not yet one} The last row of \cref{tab:yield} is an
anonymous single-shot report (``report D'') for a 33.6-kilobyte source that is not in the
repository. It does not describe the 96,694-byte Gemini entrant, and we do not assign it to Gemini by
resemblance of names, although its scale --- about 9.7 thousand completion tokens in 75--105 seconds
--- is close to that of Gemini's statement. On its own count of 7,850 code tokens it claims 81\% of visible completion and
39\% of everything processed; on our byte count, 74\% of what was generated. But its arithmetic does
not hold: it reports 33.6 kilobytes as 7,850 tokens (4.3 characters per token, not the 3.8 it states),
describes a 34.5-kilobyte prompt that matches neither the 39.7-kilobyte packet nor the 68.7-kilobyte file,
counts 3,400 reasoning tokens a model cannot observe in itself, and derives its 75--105-second
duration from an assumed token rate, so any tokens-per-second figure computed from it would be
circular. The two single-pass self-reports agree with each other in claiming that most of what a
single pass generates survives into the program; neither agrees with its own source. Until metered
usage exists (E0, \cref{sec:protocol}), the defensible summary is that reasoning-only invention by
the documented sessions kept on the order of a tenth of the text it processed, and that the
single-pass claims are a hypothesis worth metering, not a result.

\subsection{Tokens per second: what the rate measures}
\label{sec:throughput}

Session-level rates are not decoding speeds, and \cref{fig:frontier}c keeps two of them apart. The
filled points divide everything a session processed by its time to frozen source: about 104 tokens a
second for Astra (78--143), 79 for Sol (59--105) and 87 for Opus (33--200, its time being an estimate
of 15--40 minutes). The open diamonds divide only the frozen source by the same time: Astra delivered
10.3 source tokens a second (9.0--12.0), Sol 9.4 (8.2--11.0) and Opus 12.7 (6.9--24.6). Gemini's
self-reported 169 tokens a second (139--215), and the 395 source tokens a second (284--586) its stated
time would imply, are shown in grey for the reasons given above. Opus generated
about 50 tokens a second over its whole session and at least 53 a second while writing its final
20-thousand-token source message. The ratio of the two rates is the token yield again: a session
that processes a hundred tokens a second and keeps ten of them delivers ten tokens of algorithm a
second.

These rates describe a whole authoring session --- reading, clarification, reasoning and writing ---
and not a model's streaming speed; with unknown hardware and no metering, they support no
efficiency ranking between providers. They are useful for a different comparison. The engine's
emitter writes each of its pilot programs, 3.9--10.8 thousand tokens of Rust, in 4.6--7.0
milliseconds (median of 25 runs, process start included), about a million tokens a second. That
number says nothing about invention: the program was chosen beforehand, after 256 executed
candidates, and the emitter only expands a decision of 13--45 kernel tokens by a factor of 240--300.
A frontier session, a candidate-evaluating search and a compiler expanding a decision measure three
different things; \cref{tab:yield} is the only place they share a row, and the useful quantity for
any of them is cost to a useful fitted predictor, not text per second.

\subsection{What the frozen programs cost to run}

The entrants' footprints differ by more than their accuracy (\cref{fig:frontier}d). Fitting 576
times took 62 seconds for Astra, 72 for Sol and 459 for Opus on one host --- 0.11, 0.13 and 0.80
seconds per fit --- and 1,986 seconds for Gemini on another, 3.4 seconds per fit with a longest
single fit of 56 seconds. Peak memory ranged from 19 to 80 megabytes. Because the hosts differ,
Gemini's time supports no controlled speed ratio; within one host, Opus's lead in quality was bought
with six to seven times the fitting time of Sol's and Astra's. All four are complete, single-threaded,
dependency-free programs that could be deployed as they stand.

\paragraph{Amortization} Yields compare \emph{where} exploration happens, not total cost. A frontier
entrant's authoring cost is paid once and buys a program that serves every later dataset; a
data-conditioned proposer pays a training cost once and a proposal cost per dataset. Over $N$
datasets,
\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{U}}(N) &= C_{\mathrm{author}} + N\,\bigl(c_{\mathrm{fit}}^{\mathrm{U}} + c_{\mathrm{predict}}^{\mathrm{U}}\bigr),\\
C_{\mathrm{IZ}}(N) &= C_{\mathrm{history}} + C_{\mathrm{train}} + N\,\bigl(c_{\mathrm{encode}} + c_{\mathrm{propose}} + c_{\mathrm{emit}} + c_{\mathrm{fit}}^{\mathrm{IZ}} + c_{\mathrm{predict}}^{\mathrm{IZ}}\bigr),
\end{align}
where $C_{\mathrm{history}}$ is the cost of the experiments the proposer learns from. Break-even
falls at $N^\star = (C_{\mathrm{history}} + C_{\mathrm{train}} - C_{\mathrm{author}})/(c^{\mathrm{U}} -
c^{\mathrm{IZ}})$ when the per-dataset difference is positive and matched quality holds, and nowhere
otherwise. Charging the frontier model's authoring cost to every dataset while amortizing the
proposer's training would manufacture an advantage; we do neither, and unknown upstream pretraining
costs are disclosed symmetrically.

\paragraph{What the comparison shows} Reasoning in language produced four working, deployable
regression algorithms, and one of them is clearly better than the others by wins. The receipts
locate the invention in the text: roughly nine-tenths of what each documented session processed was
exploration that did not survive. And the four programs are, in substance, one design. A track-C
proposer would have nothing to discard by construction --- it commits the first program it decodes ---
so the claim worth testing is not that its yield is 100\%, which is definitional, but that it reaches
a frontier entrant's held-out quality at lower cost per useful algorithm once both are fully
amortized. That claim is gate M6 of the protocol (\cref{sec:protocol}).
