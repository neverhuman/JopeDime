\section{Can Machines Invent?}\label{sec:intro}

We specify how to test whether feedback from execution improves the first
algorithm a system proposes for a new problem. AlphaGo Zero supplies a
precedent within a fixed game: it went beyond the best human play without
seeing a human game, learning from self-play under the rules of Go
\citep{silver2017mastering}.

\paragraph{The skeptics' challenge.}
Language models retrieve existing solutions rather than find new ones,
Yann LeCun argued in March 2025: ``in their purest form, they are incapable
of inventing new things'' \citep{lecun2025bigtech}. Thomas Wolf
warned that current trends will give us ``a country of yes-men on servers''
\citep{wolf2025einstein}.

Dwarkesh Patel had asked Dario Amodei in August 2023
what to make of models with ``basically the entire corpus of human knowledge
memorized'' that had not made ``a single new connection that has led to a
discovery'' \citep{patel2023question}. In January 2025, Demis Hassabis posed
the hardest version as a benchmark for general intelligence: today's systems
``can play a game of Go at a world champion level. But could a system invent
Go?'' \citep{hassabis2025bigtech}. These challenges concern both proposing
solutions and choosing new questions; our test concerns only algorithm
proposals under human-defined tasks and metrics (\cref{sec:meaning}).

Predicting historical text does not directly reward improvement over an
incumbent. That motivates a different training signal; it does not establish
a ceiling on what a pretrained model can discover (\cref{sec:anatomy}).

\paragraph{The human challenge.}
Research effort rose while productivity declined in the industries,
products and firms examined in the 2020 study by \citet{bloom2020ideas}.
Their Moore's law example required more than 18 times as many
researchers as in the early 1970s to sustain chip-density growth.
\citet{jones2009burden} found later first inventions and greater
specialization in the inventor data he studied.
\citet{park2023disruptive} reported declining disruption on citation-based
measures of papers and patents. In life science, \citet{azoulay2019funeral}
observed increased contributions from outsiders after the premature deaths
of eminent scientists. Our question is whether execution feedback can
help a proposer depart usefully from familiar ideas.

\paragraph{From search to learning.}
Automated research systems already couple proposals to tests. The AI
Scientist \citep{lu2024aiscientist} and the AI co-scientist
\citep{gottweis2025coscientist} run agentic research loops. AlphaEvolve evolves
programs against automated evaluators \citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}.
Karpathy's autoresearch retains training changes that improve a measured
objective \citep{karpathy2026autoresearch}. Socher's \emph{Eureka Machine}
extends this vision to agents working with simulators and autonomous
laboratories \citep{socher2026bookbite,socher2026eureka}. These systems make
judgment executable.

Keeping a winning program preserves an output. Training a proposer on
those outcomes may preserve a capability that
transfers to a new problem.

\paragraph{The Zero move.}
AlphaGo learned from human expert games and searched heavily at every move
\citep{silver2016mastering}.
AlphaGo Zero learned only from games against itself, and its network alone,
with no search, rated 3,055 Elo. That is close to the 3,144 of AlphaGo Fan, the
version that beat the European champion Fan Hui
\citep{silver2016mastering,silver2017mastering}.

This motivates an analogous
goal for invention: learn from repeated program evaluations during training,
then deploy a writer with a single-candidate budget, \searchone{}
(\cref{box:searchone}). The budget is a test condition, not a
promise that every first proposal wins. \Cref{fig:emptysquare} separates
the training feedback path from the frozen writer and fitting procedure at
deployment.

\begin{explainer}[label=box:searchone]{What ``search = 1'' means}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{One inference, one candidate.} For a new problem, the program
    writer emits one complete program ($\budget = 1$), with no retries, repairs,
    best-of-$N$, reranking or weight updates.
  \item \textbf{Everything is counted,} including reasoning tokens and problem
    encoding. One inference is one generation, not one forward pass.
  \item \textbf{Declare the fitting procedure.} Fitting the emitted algorithm
    to training rows is allowed. Any internal model or hyperparameter
    selection must be disclosed and counted in its compute budget. One
    outer proposal does not establish the absence of internal search.
    Training search is priced separately in \cref{sec:price}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{A single candidate, with prior knowledge:} human-designed program
primitives and pretrained encoding components supply prior knowledge. The
Zero analogy concerns the additional training feedback from the referee.
\end{explainer}

\paragraph{Engineered intelligence.}
Each of the four pillars in \cref{sec:anatomy} is a specified component of
\EZ{} that can be inspected and tested: aim, variation, judgment and
retention. The aim is a declared task and metric. Variation comes from a
grammar-constrained program writer. Judgment is a referee that scores each
program on folds withheld from its fit. Retention uses two ratchets. A
board's incumbent changes only on strict improvement. A retrained
writer replaces the serving one only after a held-out admission test
(\cref{tab:pillars}). We call this \emph{engineered intelligence}: a
capability whose aim, judge and memory are designed to be audited,
rather than left implicit in a pretrained model. Pretrained components still
supply prior knowledge (\cref{box:searchone}). Engineering fixes what the
system is rewarded for and how its improvement is checked.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We formulate algorithm invention as a game and design a learner for it,
instantiated for tabular prediction tasks whose moves are programs in a
typed language of learning primitives.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt,label=\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{The anatomy of innovation:} four pillars (aim, variation,
    judgment and retention), with an idealized relation between proposal
    quality and the number of attempts (\cref{sec:anatomy}).
  \item \textbf{The innovation game:} a board, move, referee and opponent,
    with a reward ordering and two ratchets, that make invention playable
    on many boards (\cref{sec:game}).
  \item \textbf{The economics of algorithmic self-play:} a cost model for
    training and for the cost per verified improvement (\cref{sec:price}).
  \item \textbf{\EZ{}:} a learner with a frozen problem encoder, a grammar-constrained
    program writer and a separate problem--program map, trained by expert
    iteration on referee scores, with safeguards against reward hacking
    (\cref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{An evaluation protocol for the single-proposal claim:}
    paired comparisons with frozen algorithms whose receipts record the
    model that designed them, performance curves against agents
    allowed repeated attempts, and a test of the map's ranking against a
    program-only control (\cref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What is and is not established.}
The paper separates three kinds of evidence. The game, the learner and the
evaluation protocol are specified here. Retained admission and program-promotion
logs from later adapter training describe development behavior
(\cref{sec:method-status}). They do not measure first-proposal quality on
unseen problem families. For deployment, \cref{sec:results} specifies the
comparisons. Their outcomes are not yet reported; we claim no advantage
until they are.

\Cref{sec:anatomy} separates innovation's roles. We trace their AI building
blocks (\cref{sec:road}), then define the game and learner.
\Cref{sec:results} specifies the comparisons whose outcomes will bound the
claims in \cref{sec:meaning}.
