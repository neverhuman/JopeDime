Can machines learn to invent? The consequential test is whether experience can teach a system to propose a useful algorithm for an unseen problem before receiving feedback on that proposal. A new program is not yet new knowledge: its value must survive evaluation. We introduce EurekaZero to turn evaluated discovery experience into a transferable ability to propose algorithms. The test is the quality of the first attempt on a new problem.

The challenge is sharper than fluent generation. In a March 2025 interview, Yann LeCun questioned language models’ capacity for scientific discovery ([LeCun, 2025](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qvNCVYkHKfg)). Thomas Wolf warned that prevailing training practices could produce “a country of yes-men on servers” ([Wolf, 2025](https://thomwolf.io/blog/scientific-ai.html)). Dwarkesh Patel had posed the underlying puzzle in August 2023: why had extensive learned knowledge yielded so little demonstrated discovery ([Patel, 2023](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/dario-amodei))? Demis Hassabis set a higher bar in January 2025: “could a system invent Go?” ([Hassabis, 2025](https://www.bigtechnology.com/p/google-deepmind-ceo-demis-hassabis)). These challenges concern different capabilities, but expose a common gap: knowing the record of discovery does not establish an ability to extend it.

Human inventors also work under the weight of inherited knowledge. Research effort can rise while measured productivity falls ([Bloom et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180338)); accumulating knowledge can demand longer preparation and greater specialization ([Jones, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-937X.2008.00531.x)). In the life-science fields studied by [Azoulay et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20161574), the deaths of eminent researchers were followed by increased contributions from outsiders drawing on different intellectual traditions. These pressures motivate a second source of instruction alongside inherited knowledge: the observed consequences of new proposals.

Automated research makes this feedback operational. The AI Scientist links idea generation to experiments and reporting; the AI co-scientist organizes iterative hypothesis generation, critique, and refinement ([Lu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292v3); [Gottweis et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18864v1)). AlphaEvolve combines language models, program evaluation, and evolutionary search to discover improved algorithms ([Novikov et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13131v1)). Their repeated proposals and evaluations create experience from which a generator could learn. EurekaZero asks how much of that experience can be retained as a transferable proposal policy. Search supplies the attempts; learning should improve the next first attempt.

Go provides a concrete precedent. AlphaGo combined learning from human games, reinforcement learning, and tree search ([Silver et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961)). AlphaGo Zero subsequently learned without human game examples, using self-play, supplied rules, and search. Its final network, evaluated by choosing the highest-probability move without tree search, approached AlphaGo Fan’s playing strength in the paper’s tournament ([Silver et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24270), Figure 6). The full search-based system remained substantially stronger. Training had produced a policy that retained strong play without deployment-time tree search. Expert Iteration makes this interaction explicit: search improves a policy, and the learned policy supports stronger search ([Anthony et al., 2017](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/d8e1344e27a5b08cdfd5d027d9b8d6de-Abstract.html)).

EurekaZero formulates algorithm invention as a game. The encoded problem is the board; a complete algorithm is a move; an evaluator is the referee; and an incumbent algorithm supplies the performance target. Algorithmic self-play makes the learner’s own candidates and retained incumbents part of its training experience. The objective is to turn evaluated attempts into a better program writer across problems. At deployment, the writer commits to one complete candidate, without a generate–evaluate–revise loop. We call this budget *search = 1*, defined in Box 1. One attempt is a constraint under which to measure capability, not a promise that every attempt succeeds.

> [!CALLOUT]
>
> **Box 1. What search = 1 means**
>
> One generation produces one complete algorithm candidate. There is no execution feedback during generation, retry, repair, best-of-N selection, reranking, or deployment-time update to the generator’s weights. An invalid output counts as a failed attempt. Autoregressive generation may require many token-level forward passes; encoding and reasoning costs are included. The fixed emitted algorithm is then fitted to the problem’s training data, and its predictions are evaluated separately. Fitting may be iterative, but cannot conceal a replacement algorithm-generation loop or use the final evaluation data. This is no deployment-time candidate search, not an absence of prior knowledge, training, or computation.

Our contribution is to connect three elements: a game formulation of algorithm invention, learning across evaluated attempts, and a deployment test that exposes the quality of one proposal. The empirical comparison is deliberately focused: algorithms developed once by Claude, Codex, and Gemini to be generally competitive learning methods, alongside KumoRFM. These are reusable algorithm contestants, not a ranking of the models’ general intelligence. A separate candidate-budget comparison asks what additional problem-specific search buys. Development effort and deployment cost must therefore be accounted for separately for every entrant.

The broader possibility is that an experiment can contribute twice: once by establishing what works, and again by improving what the system proposes next. The paper develops the anatomy of innovation, the relevant learning precedents, the game’s rules and economics, the EurekaZero method, and its evaluation before considering other domains. To see why a referee changes everything, we first ask what innovation is made of.
