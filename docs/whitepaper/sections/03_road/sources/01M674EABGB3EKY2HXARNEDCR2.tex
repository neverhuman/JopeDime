\section{The Road to Zero: Five Building Blocks}\label{sec:road}

What has AI already built for each of the four pillars of
\cref{sec:anatomy}? Nearly all of it. Deep learning gave variation a voice,
program evolution and reinforcement learning built judgment loops, games
made judgment cheap, and the race for self-improvement now scales search to
swarms of agents. What none has built, to our knowledge, is taste for
invention itself: judgment learned in advance, so that the first program
written for a new problem is already strong.\footnote{Each history keeps
only the milestones the argument needs; \cref{app:history} gives the
extended version.}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Deep learning gave variation a voice}\label{sub:road-deep}

Deep learning built the variation pillar: machines that propose. Learned
representations replaced hand-written rules \citep{lecun2015deep}. AlexNet
made the case on images \citep{krizhevsky2012imagenet}, the transformer
made it general \citep{vaswani2017attention}, and scaling laws made progress
predictable \citep{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022chinchilla}.
\citet{sutton2019bitter} drew the lesson: general methods that use
computation win. Generative models then turned representations into
proposals, from text to video \citep{brooks2024sora} and code. Codex solved
28.8\% of the HumanEval programming problems with one sample, and 70.2\%
with 100 samples, counting any that passed the unit tests
\citep{chen2021codex}. That gap is \cref{eq:taste} in miniature: tries,
judged by a referee, buy what taste cannot supply.

Networks already propose discoveries that a referee outside them confirms:
protein structures that rival experiment in most cases
\citep{jumper2021alphafold},
2.2 million crystals predicted to be stable \citep{merchant2023gnome}, and
halicin, an antibiotic that then treated infections in mice
\citep{stokes2020halicin}. CLIP put images and captions in one shared space
\citep{radford2021clip}, the template for our problem--program map
(\cref{sec:method}).

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What \EZ{} takes:} representations that read a problem,
models that write code, and one space for two kinds of object.\\
\textbf{What was missing:} trained to predict the record, these models learn
history's taste: the likely, not the better.

\subsection{Machines that invent algorithms built the variation--judgment loop}\label{sub:road-programs}

Program evolution joined variation to judgment: propose a program, score
it, keep the better ones. \citet{friedberg1958learning} let a machine vary
its own instructions, \citet{holland1975adaptation} made variation and
selection a general method of adaptation, and Lenat's AM and EURISKO
searched concepts and heuristics by measures of interest
\citep{lenat1976am,lenat1983eurisko}. EURISKO takes its name from the Greek
verb behind \emph{eureka}, as ours does. \citet{koza1992genetic} closed the
loop with genetic programming: a population of programs, a fitness function
as referee and selection as coach. \emph{Genetic programming invented the
game; it lacked a player that remembers.}

Later work sharpened both halves: superoptimization
\citep{massalin1987superoptimizer}, program synthesis from examples
\citep{gulwani2011flashfill} guided by neural networks
\citep{balog2017deepcoder}, neural architecture search \citep{zoph2017nas},
and AutoML-Zero, which evolved learning algorithms from basic mathematical
operations \citep{real2020automlzero}. Then language models became the
variation operator. FunSearch found cap-set constructions beyond the best
known \citep{romeraparedes2024funsearch}: an answer to the skeptics of
\cref{sec:intro}, bought with search. Lion, an optimizer found by program
search, went into production at Google \citep{chen2023lion}. AlphaEvolve
multiplied two $4\times4$ complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications,
improving on Strassen's algorithm in that setting after 56 years
\citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}. MLEvolve carries the loop into
machine-learning engineering \citep{mlevolve2026}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What \EZ{} takes:} programs as moves, and the
generate--judge loop.\\
\textbf{What was missing:} retention flows into an archive, a library or a
memory, not into the program writer. Even DreamCoder's cross-task learning
guides search rather than replacing it \citep{ellis2021dreamcoder}, so every
new problem starts its search over.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning turned retention into taste}\label{sub:road-rl}

Reinforcement learning turns a referee's verdicts into a better player.
\citet{bellman1957dp} gave it an equation of value. Samuel's checkers
program improved by playing a copy of itself \citep{samuel1959checkers},
TD-Gammon reached near the best human backgammon players by self-play
\citep{tesauro1995tdgammon}, and DQN learned Atari games from pixels
\citep{mnih2015dqn}. AlphaGo learned from human games and searched at every
move \citep{silver2016mastering}; AlphaGo Zero learned by self-play alone
\citep{silver2017mastering}. AlphaZero mastered three games with one
algorithm \citep{silver2018general}, MuZero learned the rules as well
\citep{schrittwieser2020muzero}, and AlphaStar's league of mutually
exploiting agents reached Grandmaster level in StarCraft~II
\citep{vinyals2019alphastar}.

The key concept is expert iteration \citep{anthony2017expert}: search is the
teacher, the network is the student, and in the end the student alone plays
strongly. AlphaGo Zero's raw network, with no search, nearly matched the
version that had beaten a professional (\cref{sec:intro}); Box~2 shows how.
Language models now learn the same way, from human preferences
\citep{christiano2017preferences,ouyang2022instructgpt} and from rule-based
checks of answers, as in DeepSeek-R1 \citep{guo2025deepseekr1}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What \EZ{} takes:} the learning rule, and proof that a
network can absorb search.\\
\textbf{What was missing:} the prize. Verifiable rewards ask whether an
answer is right; no game here pays for beating the best known algorithm on
a problem the player has never seen.

\begin{explainer}{Box 2 --- Self-play in 90 seconds}
A player starts with only the rules and plays itself. A short search
before each move finds better moves than instinct would, and the referee
scores each finished game. The network learns to propose the moves the
search chose (its policy) and to predict the winner (its value), so the
next search is stronger. Over millions of games, search is distilled into
intuition \citep{anthony2017expert,silver2017mastering}.
\emph{What if the board were a problem, and the move an invention?}
\end{explainer}

\subsection{Gamification made judgment cheap}\label{sub:road-games}

Games built the judgment pillar at scale. \citet{shannon1950chess} chose
chess because its moves and its goal are sharply defined. The common task
framework made machine-learning research a game: shared data, enrolled
competitors and a referee who scores every entry on a sealed test set
\citep{donoho2017fifty}, as in the Netflix Prize \citep{bennett2007netflix}
and ImageNet \citep{russakovsky2015imagenet}. People played science too.
Foldit players beat automated methods on hard protein puzzles
\citep{cooper2010foldit} and solved a protease structure that earlier
attempts had not \citep{khatib2011protease}; EteRNA players, whose designs
were tested in a laboratory, found RNA design rules \citep{lee2014eterna}.

Then machines played problems. AlphaTensor's TensorGame beat the best known
matrix-multiplication algorithms for several sizes
\citep{fawzi2022alphatensor}, and AlphaDev's AssemblyGame found sorting
routines now in the LLVM C++ library \citep{mankowitz2023alphadev}.
AlphaGeometry learned olympiad geometry without human demonstrations
\citep{trinh2024alphageometry}. AlphaProof, refereed by the Lean proof
checker, reached silver-medal standard at the 2024 International
Mathematical Olympiad alongside AlphaGeometry~2 \citep{hubert2026alphaproof}.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What \EZ{} takes:} the recipe for a game: rules, a referee,
a score and a sealed test.\\
\textbf{What was missing:} each board was built by hand for one problem
family, and its player searches on every instance; AlphaProof trained for
days on variants of each hard problem. AlphaDev made sorting a game; \EZ{}
makes the problem itself part of the board (\cref{sec:game}).

\subsection{The race for self-improvement scaled search}\label{sub:road-self}

The race for self-improvement scales all four pillars, so far with search.
\citet{good1966speculations} foresaw an ``intelligence explosion'', and
\citet{schmidhuber2003goedel} specified a G\"odel machine that rewrites its
own code once it proves the rewrite useful. STOP improves the scaffold that calls a language model, including
itself, and its authors note: ``Since the language models themselves are
not altered, this is not full recursive self-improvement''
\citep{zelikman2024stop}. The Darwin G\"odel Machine's self-editing agents
raised its SWE-bench score from 20.0\% to 50.0\% \citep{zhang2025dgm}, and
AlphaEvolve cut Gemini's training time by 1\%
\citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}.

Language models also play themselves. SPIN plays its previous iteration
\citep{chen2024spin}, Absolute Zero proposes and solves coding tasks under a
code executor \citep{zhao2025absolutezero}, and STP pairs a conjecturer with
a Lean prover \citep{dong2025stp}. Such self-play plateaus as the
conjecturer games its reward, \citet{bailey2026sgs} report, unless a guide
keeps it on track. Our closest neighbors learn to discover. EvoTune
fine-tunes on what its own evolutionary search finds \citep{evotune2025},
and TTT-Discover trains on the one problem at hand, at a few hundred
dollars per problem \citep{tttdiscover2026}. Evolution Fine-Tuning learns
from \num{156000} search trajectories across 371 tasks \citep{eft2026}, and
DiscoRL meta-learned a reinforcement-learning rule that beat human-designed
ones \citep{oh2025discorl}. Each still searches on a new problem, or finds
one general method and reuses it, as our frontier contestants do
(\cref{sec:results}).

Research itself is becoming a swarm. The AI Scientist automates ideas,
experiments and papers \citep{lu2024aiscientist}, and the AI co-scientist
ranks agents' hypotheses in an Elo tournament
\citep{gottweis2025coscientist}. Karpathy's autoresearch, released in March
2026, runs about 100 experiments overnight and keeps what improves
\citep{karpathy2026autoresearch}. In May 2026, Recursive raised
\$650 million to build AI that improves itself
\citep{recursive2026techcrunch}. Their referees are our boards
(\cref{sec:anatomy}).

Self-improvement comes in three strengths: better external algorithms,
better components, and a better ability to improve, with verified
discoveries per unit of compute rising across generations. Our evidence is
at the first (\cref{sec:meaning}).

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What \EZ{} takes:} the destination, and the safety frame:
verify every improvement before keeping it, and sandbox every program.\\
\textbf{What was missing:} each approach here pays for judgment per problem,
learns within one problem's search, or plateaus. None is built to make a
first move strong enough to need no search.

\Cref{tab:roads} sets the closest work on one axis: what a new problem costs
in tries, and where the taste that cuts that cost comes from. Two neighbors
also answer in one try. TabPFN, MotherNet and KumoRFM handle a new dataset
without task-specific training
\citep{hollmann2025tabpfn,muller2025mothernet,kumorfm2025}, but return
predictions or a fixed network's weights. \EZ{} returns a readable
algorithm, and learned to write it by beating incumbents. Absolute Zero and
STP learn by self-play, but their prize is correctness. Ours is beating the
best known program, which is why the game needs an opponent
(\cref{sec:game}).

\begin{keyidea}
Every pillar has been built. What has not been built, to our knowledge, is
a player whose taste for invention was learned from a referee, so that a
new problem needs one try.
\end{keyidea}

To build that player, we first make invention a game.
