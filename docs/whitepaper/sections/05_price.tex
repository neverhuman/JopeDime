\section{The Price of Play}\label{sec:price}
% Reader question: Can anyone afford to play it?
% One-sentence answer: The independent-try model gives mean attempt cost divided
%   by win probability; full-run prices and deployment savings still need evidence.
% Core points advanced: 3 (search = 1) and 2 (search length is the
%   reciprocal of taste); supports 1 (cheap replay is what invention lacks).
% Exhibits: Equation 2 (eq:bill), Box 3. The pilot-cost figure (fig:price) lives in Results.
% Unsupported supplied cost projections are omitted pending reproducible calculations.

What would it cost to play the innovation game? A complete ledger counts
encoding, input tokens, generation, repairs, referee execution, updates and
operating costs. Replay cost constrains the game (\cref{sec:game}), but a
reproducible full-run price is still pending (\cref{sub:price-run}).
We separate planning assumptions from the measurements required in
\cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{The bill in one line}\label{sub:price-bill}

For independent attempts with fixed win probability and cost distribution,
expected cost per win is mean cost per attempt divided by that probability
(\cref{eq:taste}):
\begin{equation}
  \Cwin = \frac{\Cattempt}{\winprob}.
  \label{eq:bill}
\end{equation}
Here $\Cattempt$ is the mean incurred cost of one candidate attempt,
including input processing, reasoning, generation, checks and referee
execution. Work spent on failures remains in the mean. A repair that emits
a revised candidate is another attempt; charge its work once. The
$\winprob$ is the probability that one attempt beats the incumbent.
It factors as $\winprob = q_{\text{run}}\,q_{\text{beat}}$,
the share of moves that run times the share of those that win. A move that
runs and loses still costs money, so we divide by $\winprob$, not by the survival
rate. Generating a token costs about 2~FLOPs per active parameter, and
training on one about~6 \citep{kaplan2020scaling}. Cost projections require reproducible inputs and calculations (\cref{sub:price-run}).

For an observed batch, cost per verified win is the total ledger divided
by verified wins, including update and operating costs where applicable.
This accounting does not require independent attempts. Adaptive search
still needs empirical cost and attempt curves; a batch with no verified
win has no finite observed cost per win.

The bill has two ledgers. Training pays for every attempted candidate,
including failures; deployment pays for encoding, one proposal and its
fitting procedure, with no repair. Fixed-weight agent loops instead buy
evaluation during task-specific search; a trained proposer may amortize
some of that work across later tasks. Whether the saving repays training
requires a measured crossover, still pending in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Wasted moves}\label{sub:price-waste}

An attempt can fail before or after it becomes a valid competitor.
A stillborn attempt fails to produce a usable program under the declared
protocol: generation is incomplete, parsing or type-checking fails,
execution exceeds its limits or crashes, or outputs fail predeclared
validity checks. An also-ran is valid but does not beat the incumbent. The pilot funnel planned for \cref{sec:results} (\cref{fig:price}, panel~a) traces
those stages and the compute spent before each failed attempt stops. If 90\% of attempts fail to improve the incumbent, the independent-try
model requires ten attempts per improvement in expectation. A typed language
can exclude invalid structures before fitting (\cref{sec:game}). Runtime,
resource and output failures may consume part or all of the fit, and their
incurred cost must be counted. Whether also-rans dominate is a pilot question. Learning, a changed search
procedure or a changed opponent can affect the measured win rate; isolate
the effect attributed to the writer.

\subsection{The correction tax and the reasoning dial}\label{sub:price-tax}

This subsection and \cref{sub:price-run} concern language-model writers and
the agent comparators of \cref{sub:results-headtohead,sub:results-search};
the reference network of \cref{sec:method}, writer and map together, has
27.8 million parameters and writes at most 128 grammar tokens. Small models are cheap per token and can be dear per win. For generation
alone, compute per token falls about 190-fold from a hypothetical dense 1.5T writer to
an 8B one. With equal token counts and compute unit costs, the smaller
writer can tolerate a win rate about 190 times lower before that
component's cost per win matches. If it needs three times the tokens per
attempt, the generation-only ratio falls to about 60. Repair turns also
re-read a growing context. Encoding and referee costs further change the
full-cost ratio, so the threshold for the complete bill in \cref{eq:bill}
requires all components to be counted.

Reasoning is the second dial. Reasoning tokens may cut stillborn moves, but
each is billed as output. The planned pilot (\cref{fig:price}, panel~b) compares
cost per win over writer size and reasoning effort. A U-shaped curve is the hypothesis; the
measurement decides where, and whether, the minimum sits.

\subsection{Rewarding useful hallucination}\label{sub:price-hallucination}

A familiar solution can be a useful prior. Matching the incumbent's score
earns a tie; promotion requires improvement under the same protocol. A
\emph{useful hallucination} here is an unfamiliar proposal whose value the
referee verifies (\cref{box:hallucination}). Possible exploration controls include temperature
and entropy schedules, a curriculum past known solutions, and
quality-diversity archives \citep{lehman2011novelty,mouret2015mapelites}.
Any novelty bonus must preserve the reward ordering in \cref{sec:game}.
These are design options. In the reference procedure, exploration comes
from 64 drafts sampled at temperature~1, 16 random exploration
programs added to them, and verified search seeded with the result
(\cref{sub:method-selfplay}). A measured exploration study
must declare its controls and budget share and count unsuccessful attempts;
its quality--cost trade-off belongs with the results (\cref{sec:results}).

\begin{explainer}[label=box:hallucination]{What a referee verifies}
  An unfamiliar program is a conjecture; the referee checks whether it
  runs and improves the declared score. Verification establishes that
  bounded result. A claim of discovery also needs comparison with known
  methods (\cref{sec:meaning}). AlphaGo's Move~37 was unlikely by its own
  estimate and won its game (\cref{sub:anatomy-history}). A referee does
  not make strange moves good. Its reliability and execution cost must be
  established for the task (\cref{sec:method}).
\end{explainer}

\subsection{How many games?}\label{sub:price-games}

Three quantities differ by orders of magnitude.
\textbf{Tries to find one improvement:} $1/\winprob$ on average (\cref{eq:taste}).
For independent tries with fixed $\winprob = 1\%$, 69 attempts reach even
odds and 299 reach a 95\% probability of at least one win in that model.
Report empirical attempt curves for adaptive or correlated proposals.
\textbf{Experience to train a transferable player:} AlphaGo Zero generated
4.9 million self-play games in about three days \citep{silver2017mastering},
and AlphaZero played 44 million games of chess in about nine hours
\citep{silver2017chess,silver2018general}. An illustrative grid of 10,000
problems, 100 rounds and 8 attempts per problem per round gives 8 million
attempts, the same order.
The grid counts work, not learning: the number of problem families matters
more, and pilot learning curves, read only within their measured range, must
decide.
\textbf{Precision to plan the test:} illustratively, 150 independent
units at a win rate near 60\% give an approximate 95\% interval of
$\pm 8$ points. The actual study must compute uncertainty over independent
families, rather than treating every dataset as an independent observation.

\subsection{Pricing evidence still required}\label{sub:price-run}

Mixture-of-experts writers use compute on active parameters but need memory
for all parameters: Kimi~K2 has 32B active of 1T, while DeepSeek-V3 has 37B
active of 671B \citep{kimiteam2025k2,deepseekv3}. Resident one-byte weights
for 1T parameters alone require at least 13 H100 SXM GPUs with 80~GB each
\citep{nvidia2026h100}; runtime state needs additional memory. This memory
floor is separate from the cost estimates omitted below.

A cost projection needs a pinned model and hardware configuration, dated
prices, measured or justified utilization, a reproducible calculation, and
an explicit accounting boundary. Those inputs are not yet recorded, so this
paper reports no cost projections; they remain a measurement task.

\begin{keyidea}
  Cost per verified improvement depends on both the cost of an attempt
  and the probability that it wins.
\end{keyidea}

Any future projection of a fixed attempt budget must be converted to cost
per win using a measured win rate and compared under a common task mix,
incumbent standard and verification rule. Under \cref{eq:bill}'s
fixed-probability model, doubling $\winprob$ halves expected cost per win
only if mean cost per attempt stays fixed.

The reference checkpoint in \cref{sec:method} uses a compact network with
27.8 million parameters and at most 128 grammar-constrained tokens, with no
repair in the proposed single-candidate deployment protocol. Its measured
costs must be reported separately from any future scaling projection.

The method that follows aims to raise $\winprob$ through training. Whether
it succeeds must be tested on one proposal under the stated deployment
budget.
